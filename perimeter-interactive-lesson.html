5.2 Calculating the perimeter for rectangular shapes

We are learning to calculate the perimeter of rectangles using familiar metric units, and to solve practical problems.

Success criteria
Big idea 1

What is perimeter?

Perimeter is the distance all the way around the outside edge of a shape. You find it by adding the length of every side.

Walk around the edge

Click Take a step to walk around the rectangle one side at a time, and watch the running total build up. That total is the perimeter.

TeacherSay it out loud. Ask students to chant the running total with each click: "3, then 5, then 8, then 10." Landing back on the starting corner is the moment to name it as the perimeter.

A square

All four sides are equal. 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8 cm

A rectangle

Opposite sides are equal. 3 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 10 cm

Big idea 2

A faster way: the formula

Because opposite sides of a rectangle are equal, you only need two measurements. Add the length and the width, then double it.
Perimeter = 2 × ( length + width )

A square is a special rectangle where the length and the width are the same, so you can also do side × 4.

Units you will use: mm, cm, m, and km. Always write the unit in your answer.

TeacherAsk the noticing question. Set the length to 8 and slide the width from 1 to 8. Students should notice the shape becomes a square exactly when length and width match, and that adding 1 to the width adds 2 to the perimeter, not 1.
Quick check

Perimeter or area?

Perimeter goes around the edge. Area covers the inside. Decide which one each job needs.

TeacherThumbs version. This also runs as the original whole-class routine: read each one aloud, thumbs up for perimeter, thumbs down for area. Use the board version first, then let students confirm on screen.

Spot the mistake

A rectangle is 8 cm by 4 cm. Two students worked out the perimeter. Click whoever you think is correct.

TeacherName the error. Student A has found the area, not the perimeter. Push for the reason, not just the answer: multiplying length by width fills the inside, while perimeter only travels the boundary.

🟠 Amber Reasoning Working backwards

Part 1: Calculate each perimeter

These rectangles are drawn at different angles and sizes. Only two measurements are given, so use the formula.

Type each perimeter, then press Check

Part 2: Working backwards

Now you know the perimeter and you need a missing side. Undo the steps.

Worked example. A rectangle has a perimeter of 22 cm and a width of 4 cm. How long is it?
In Green you added the two measurements, then doubled. Now you undo that.
22 ÷ 2 = 11, so length + width = 11.
11 − 4 = 7. The length is 7 cm.

Part 3: Draw your own

Using a ruler, draw and label three different rectangles in your maths book that each have a perimeter of 24 cm. Type each one here to check it before you draw.

✓ Answers
  • a 16 cm by 2 cm: 2 × 18 = 36 cm
  • b 10 cm by 4 cm: 2 × 14 = 28 cm
  • c 11 cm by 3 cm: 2 × 14 = 28 cm
  • d 20 cm by 5 cm: 2 × 25 = 50 cm
  • e 12 cm by 10 cm: 2 × 22 = 44 cm
  • Same perimeter: rectangles b and c, both 28 cm, even though they are different shapes.
  • Square, perimeter 36 cm: 36 ÷ 4 = 9 cm on each side.
  • Perimeter 30 cm, width 5 cm: 30 ÷ 2 = 15, then 15 − 5 = 10 cm.
  • Perimeter 24 cm, one side 9 cm: the opposite side is also 9 cm. 24 ÷ 2 = 12, then 12 − 9 = 3, so the other three sides are 9 cm, 3 cm, and 3 cm.
  • Perimeter 24 cm rectangles: any pair that adds to 12, such as 11 and 1, 10 and 2, 9 and 3, 8 and 4, or 7 and 5.
TeacherThe halving step is the hurdle. Many students subtract the known side from the full perimeter first. Insist on halving before subtracting, and have them check by adding all four sides back up. Rectangles b and c are the matched pair at 28 cm, which is a good prompt for "same perimeter does not mean same shape".
🔴 Red Problem solving Worded problems

Read each problem carefully and show your working in your maths book. Decide first whether you are finding a perimeter or a missing side.

1. Rabbit enclosure

Mrs Staples is building a rectangular rabbit enclosure. The enclosure measures 4 m by 5 m. How many metres of fence does she need?

2. Playground

The perimeter of a rectangular playground is 36 m. If the length of the park is 6 m, what is the width of the park?

3. Year 5 vegetable garden

The Year 5 vegetable garden is a rectangle 8 m long and 3 m wide. The gardening club wants to run a low timber edge all the way around the outside.

  1. How many metres of timber edging will they need?
  2. Timber edging costs $12 per metre. What will the edging cost altogether?

4. How many possibilities?

A rectangular garden has a perimeter of 20 metres. Its length is longer than its width. The length and width are both a whole number of metres. What could the length and width of the garden be? Can you find all possibilities?

There is more than one answer. Work through the widths in order so you do not miss any.

Type each answer, then press Check

✓ Answers
  • 1. Rabbit enclosure: 2 × (5 + 4) = 2 × 9 = 18 m of fence.
  • 2. Playground: 36 ÷ 2 = 18, then 18 − 6 = 12 m wide.
  • 3a. Timber edging: 2 × (8 + 3) = 2 × 11 = 22 m.
  • 3b. Cost: 22 × $12 = $264.
  • 4. All possibilities: the length and width must add to 10, so there are 4 of them: 9 m by 1 m, 8 m by 2 m, 7 m by 3 m, and 6 m by 4 m. 5 m by 5 m is left out because that is a square, so the length would not be longer than the width.
TeacherQuestion 4 is the stretch. The proof that there are exactly four is the real learning, not the list. Ask how they know they have found them all, and look for students who work systematically from a width of 1 upwards. The 5 by 5 case is worth a whole-class discussion, since it hinges on "longer than", not "at least as long as".
